# Automated Detection and Geospatial Mapping of Housing Development Bottlenecks in Connecticut's Fourth District
#### AI-Generated Research Plan for the Housing Bottleneck Mapper Project
#### Sierra Mendoza  |  Greenwich High School  |  July 2026
## Introduction
Connecticut's Fourth District faces a well-documented shortage of housing supply, particularly affordable and multi-family housing, in towns from Bridgeport to Greenwich. The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act was designed to speed up the pipeline that turns a proposed housing project into an occupied building, but the individual steps in that pipeline — local permitting, zoning review, funding approval, environmental review, and labor availability — are recorded only in scattered, town-specific public documents: zoning board minutes, building permit filings, and HUD program records. No single source currently tells a legislative office, in aggregate, where and why projects are actually getting stuck.
This mirrors a pattern seen in other complex systems: the outcome of interest (a housing project's timeline, like a cell's disease state) is rarely explained by a single isolated factor. It results from interacting conditions across permitting, zoning, funding, environmental review, and labor. A tool that looks at delay causes one document at a time, the way a legislative staffer currently must, will tend to notice whichever individual case is most visible or most recently in the news, rather than the full, systematic pattern across a whole district.
Natural language processing (NLP) and text classification make it possible, in principle, to read through thousands of heterogeneous civic records automatically, tag the cause of each recorded delay, and aggregate those tags into a district-wide picture. The central technical challenge is that these records are inconsistent in format, terminology, and completeness across towns, and delay causes are frequently mixed rather than singular. If this project succeeds, it produces both a concrete accountability tool for one Congressional office and a general, reusable template: the same collect-read-classify-visualize pattern could be applied to other legislative accountability questions (transportation permitting, environmental review, other housing legislation) in other districts.
## Objective
The primary objective of this research is to design and build a document-classification and geospatial-analytics pipeline that automatically extracts and classifies the cause of housing development delays from public records in Connecticut's Fourth District, and to use that pipeline to evaluate whether measured project timelines shortened following passage of the ROAD to Housing Act.
I expect that manual, anecdotal review of housing records under-represents the true mix of delay causes and over-weights whichever cases receive public attention, while a systematic classification pipeline applied to the full record will surface a more complete and more evenly-sampled picture of where bottlenecks occur. I further expect that, if the ROAD Act is having its intended effect, projects filed after its effective date will show a measurably shorter average time-to-resolution than comparable projects filed before it, and that this project can produce evidence either supporting or complicating that expectation.
## Research Questions
* Can NLP and machine-learning models reliably classify the cause of a housing project delay (permitting, zoning, funding, environmental, or labor) from heterogeneous public text sources, at an accuracy sufficient for policy use?
* What is the geographic and categorical distribution of delay causes across CT-4 towns (Bridgeport, Stamford, Norwalk, Darien, Greenwich)?
* Did average project timelines measurably shorten following passage of the ROAD to Housing Act, and can this be established with reasonable statistical confidence from the assembled record?
* Does an automated classification pipeline generalize across towns whose public records differ in format, completeness, and terminology?
* As a secondary aim, can automatically generated policy briefs communicate these findings accurately and usefully to legislative staff, as validated by direct feedback from the Congressman's office?
## Methodology
### 1) Assemble a multi-town public records corpus.
Build a Python-based collection pipeline that pulls publicly available records for the five target towns: zoning and planning board agendas and meeting minutes, building/permit portal filings, and HUD or state affordable-housing program filings. Where towns provide open APIs or downloadable datasets, ingest directly; where records exist only as PDFs or scanned minutes on town websites, build a scraper/downloader plus an OCR step. Every record is stored with its source town, document type, and filing date, preserving provenance so that any later classification result can be traced back to the original document.
### 2) Construct a labeled ground-truth sample.
Because no existing dataset labels "housing delay cause," manually read and hand-label a representative sample of roughly 150-300 documents or project records, assigning one or more delay categories (permitting, zoning, funding, environmental, labor) and, where determinable, key dates such as application filed, delay start, and resolution. This hand-labeled sample is the objective standard against which every automated method in later steps is checked, in the same way a known set of correlated features anchors evaluation in a simulated dataset.
### 3) Build an information-extraction layer.
Implement an NLP preprocessing pipeline that pulls structured facts out of unstructured town records: project or parcel names, filing dates, referenced agencies, and delay-indicating language (for example, "continued pending," "environmental review," "awaiting funding approval"). Use a combination of rule-based pattern matching (regular expressions and keyword dictionaries built from the hand-labeled sample) together with pretrained language-model embeddings, so the extraction step does not depend on any single brittle heuristic.
### 4) Develop the delay-cause classification model.
Train and compare a small set of text classifiers, ranging from a TF-IDF plus logistic-regression baseline to a fine-tuned transformer-based classifier, to assign a delay-cause label to each project record. Because a single project can be delayed by more than one interacting cause, this is treated as a multi-label classification problem rather than a forced single category. Models are tuned using the hand-labeled sample, with a held-out portion reserved for testing.
### 5) Reconstruct project timelines and evaluate ROAD Act impact.
For every project with sufficient date information, reconstruct a timeline from initial filing to resolution, and separate projects into "pre-ROAD-Act" and "post-ROAD-Act" cohorts based on filing date relative to the legislation's effective date. Compare timeline length and delay-cause composition across the two cohorts using appropriate statistical tests, to assess whether the legislation is associated with a measurable change in approval speed.
### 6) Build the geospatial bottleneck dashboard.
Aggregate classified, geotagged project records into an interactive map of CT-4 that a user can filter by town, delay type, and time period, so patterns — for example, a concentration of environmental-review delays in one town, or funding delays clustered around a particular period — become visible at a glance rather than buried inside individual filings.
### 7) Generate automated policy briefs.
Build a templated natural-language-generation module that turns the classified, aggregated data for a chosen town or hearing topic into a short, readable brief: which delay type is most common, how timelines compare before and after the ROAD Act, and which towns most need attention. Every brief is checked for accuracy against the underlying data before it is shared outside the project.
### 8) Evaluate performance, robustness, and real-world usefulness.
Assess the full pipeline along four dimensions: classification performance (precision, recall, and F1 against the hand-labeled test set), generalization across towns with differing record-keeping practices, statistical confidence in the pre/post-ROAD-Act comparison, and direct feedback from congressional staff on whether the dashboard and briefs are accurate and useful for their work. The model and interface are iterated on based on that feedback throughout the pilot.
